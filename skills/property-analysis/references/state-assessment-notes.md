# State assessment notes: assessed value versus market value

**Read this first.** These notes summarize the main rules as of 2026. Rules
change: legislatures change caps, counties reassess on their own schedules,
and ratios move every year. Before you rely on any rule or ratio below,
check the county assessor's or property appraiser's own website, and cite
that site and the roll year. Where a county publishes a sales ratio study
or an equalization ratio, use it rather than a state-level rule of thumb.

General rules:
- **Assessed value** is the value the county uses for tax. It may be a
  fraction of market value by law, capped for owner-occupied homes, or stale
  between reappraisals.
- **Market value** ("just value", "full cash value", "fair market value")
  is some counties' own estimate of what the property would sell for. When
  the assessor publishes it, prefer it to assessed value.
- A recent arm's-length sale price is the best market evidence.
- Homestead caps affect only the owner's primary residence. A second home in
  the same state is usually assessed closer to market.

| State | Basis of assessment | Caps and quirks | What to do |
|---|---|---|---|
| **FL** | County property appraisers publish "just value" (market) and "assessed value" each year. | **Save Our Homes**: assessed value of a homestead can rise at most 3% a year or by the CPI change, whichever is lower. Long-held homesteads can be assessed far below just value. Portability lets owners carry part of the benefit to a new home. Non-homestead property has a 10% annual cap (school taxes excepted). | Use just value, not assessed value. Say "Save Our Homes applies" for a homestead. A second home is non-homestead. |
| **CA** | Assessed at purchase price (base year value) plus at most 2% a year. | **Proposition 13** (1978). Reassessed only on a change of ownership or new construction. A home bought decades ago can be assessed at a small fraction of market. **Prop 19** (2021) changed parent-child transfer and base-year portability rules. | Use the purchase year and a recent comparable sale or a market estimate. Never treat a long-held CA assessment as market. |
| **NY** | Varies by municipality. NYC assesses 1-3 family homes (Class 1) at 6% of market value with annual increase caps (6%/yr, 20% over 5 yrs); Class 2 co-ops and condos are valued as if rental income property, well below sale prices. Outside NYC, each town sets a uniform percentage; the state publishes equalization rates. | NYC market value on the Notice of Property Value is also often below sale prices for co-ops and condos. Towns may go decades without reappraisal. | In NYC use the DOF "market value" and adjust co-ops and condos upward using sales. Outside NYC divide assessed value by the town's state equalization rate. |
| **TX** | Appraisal districts appraise at market value each year. | Homestead appraised value can rise at most 10% a year. As of 2026, a temporary 20% annual cap also applies to non-homestead real property valued at about $5M or less (tax years 2024 to 2026; check the Comptroller's site for whether it was extended). Owners often protest values down. No state income tax, so property tax is high and values are watched. | Use "market value" on the appraisal district record, not "appraised" or "taxable". |
| **VA** | Localities assess at 100% of fair market value. Cities and large counties usually reassess every year or two; other counties every 3 to 4 years, and counties of 50,000 people or fewer may wait 5 or 6 years (Code of Virginia 58.1-3250 to 58.1-3252). | Assessments can lag the market by one to several years; check the locality's last reassessment date. No homestead cap. | Assessed value is a fair first estimate; adjust with recent sales in hot markets. |
| **MA** | Assessed at 100% of full and fair cash value; state certifies values every five years with interim adjustments. | Proposition 2 1/2 limits the tax levy, not individual values. Values set as of January 1 of the prior year. | Assessed value is close to market with a one-year lag. |
| **IL** | Cook County assesses residential property at 10% of market value; the rest of the state at 33 1/3%. Then a state equalization factor ("multiplier") applies. | Cook County reassesses one third of the county each year on a triennial cycle. Exemptions reduce equalized value. | Use the assessor's published "market value" where shown. Otherwise divide assessed value by the level of assessment (0.10 in Cook, 0.333 elsewhere). Check how old the triennial value is. |
| **NJ** | Assessed at a municipal percentage of market; the state publishes an "average ratio" (Chapter 123 ratio) per municipality each year. | Many towns go years without revaluation, so ratios drift far from 100%. | Divide assessed value by the municipality's current average ratio to estimate market. |
| **CT** | Assessed at 70% of fair market value as of the last revaluation. | Revaluation at least every five years. Values can be stale between revaluations. | Divide assessed value by 0.70, then consider the age of the revaluation. |
| **WA** | Assessed at 100% of true and fair value, revalued annually. | Senior and disabled exemptions can lower taxable value, not market value. | Assessed value is close to market with a short lag. |

## Other patterns to check in any state

- **Use-value (current-use) assessment** for farm, forest and open-space land:
  assessed on its farming or timber income, far below market. Common in most
  states (for example Florida's agricultural classification, Virginia land
  use, New Jersey farmland assessment).
- **Conservation easements** lower a parcel's value permanently. They are
  also a sign of philanthropic intent.
- **Exemptions** (homestead, senior, veteran) lower taxable value. They do
  not change market value. Some reveal age or veteran status; do not report
  such personal details unless relevant and allowed by `ethics-and-privacy`.
- **Condos and co-ops**: co-op units in NYC and elsewhere may not appear as
  separate parcels. The building is the parcel. Use sales records and news.
- **Commercial property**: often valued on income. Ownership sits in LLCs.
  The prospect's share is unknown without a filing that shows it.
